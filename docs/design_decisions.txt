=======================================================================
PART 3.1 DELIVERABLE -- design_decisions.txt
Dimensional model design decisions, with the evidence behind each
=======================================================================

Every decision below is answered against the Part 2 measurements in
docs/query_analysis.txt rather than against general principle. Where this
design departs from the brief's suggested table list, the departure is
stated and defended rather than made silently.

Kimball's four steps, applied in order:
  1. Select the business process ..... clinical encounters and their billing
  2. Declare the grain ............... one row per encounter (Decision 1)
  3. Identify the dimensions ......... Decision 2
  4. Identify the facts .............. Decision 3


=======================================================================
DECISION 1: FACT TABLE GRAIN
=======================================================================

CHOICE: Option A -- one row per encounter. fact_encounters = 300,000 rows.

WHY

The evidence is Q2. Joining the two junction tables produced 2,060,000
intermediate rows from 300,000 encounters, and summing money in that shape
reported $5,752.3M against a true $838.7M -- a 6.9x overstatement with no
error raised.

Options B and C bake that defect permanently into the warehouse:

  Option B (one row per diagnosis within an encounter) -> ~858,000 rows.
  An encounter with 3 diagnoses appears 3 times. SUM(allowed_amount) is
  now wrong by default, for every user, forever. The only correct way to
  total revenue becomes SUM(DISTINCT ...) or a subquery -- which is to say
  the obvious query is the wrong query.

  Option C (one row per procedure) -> ~721,000 rows. Same failure.

Encounter grain is also the only grain at which all four business questions
can be answered:

  Q1 counts encounters and distinct patients ....... encounter grain
  Q3 counts inpatient discharges and readmissions .. encounter grain
  Q4 sums allowed_amount per claim ................. encounter grain (1:1)
  Q2 needs diagnosis x procedure pairs ............. bridge tables

Three of four questions want encounter grain. The fourth is served by
bridges without dragging the other three down with it.

THE GENERAL RULE THIS ILLUSTRATES

Grain should be the finest level at which the measures are still additive.
allowed_amount is additive per encounter. It is NOT additive per diagnosis
-- there is no such thing as "the revenue of a diagnosis" when a visit has
four of them and one bill. Choosing a grain finer than your measures makes
every measure semi-additive at best and wrong at worst.


=======================================================================
DECISION 2: DIMENSION TABLES
=======================================================================

Six dimensions. Two of the brief's suggestions are deliberately not built,
and one is added.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------
dim_date  (~1,100 rows, one-time load, covers 2023-01-01 .. 2025-12-31)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
  date_key INT PK        -- YYYYMMDD, e.g. 20240612. Human-readable and
                            sorts correctly as an integer.
  full_date, year, quarter, month, month_name, month_abbr,
  calendar_month ('2024-06'), day_of_month, day_of_week, day_name,
  is_weekend, week_of_year, day_of_year

  WHY IT EXISTS AT ALL, when encounters already has a DATETIME:
  Q1 and Q4 both spend real time on DATE_FORMAT(encounter_date,'%Y-%m'),
  which recomputes a month string for 300,000 rows on every execution and
  makes idx_encounter_date unusable for grouping. A date dimension stores
  that string once per day instead of once per encounter -- computed 1,100
  times at load, not 300,000 times per query. It also supplies calendar
  attributes SQL cannot derive (fiscal periods, holidays) as the warehouse
  grows.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------
dim_patient  (50,000 rows, SCD Type 2 structure)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
  patient_key INT PK     -- surrogate
  patient_id INT         -- natural key from source
  mrn, first_name, last_name, full_name, date_of_birth, gender,
  gender_desc ('Female' / 'Male' / 'Unknown')
  effective_from, effective_to, is_current   -- SCD2 machinery

  NOTE ON AGE: age is deliberately NOT stored here. A patient's age band
  changes with the calendar, not with any source event, so storing it in
  the dimension would silently go stale and would corrupt historical
  reporting. Age at the time of care is a property of the ENCOUNTER, so
  patient_age_years and patient_age_band live on the fact row (Decision 3).
  This is the standard Kimball treatment and it is the one place this
  design differs meaningfully from the brief's hint ("age_group" listed
  under dim_patient).

-----------------------------------------------------------------------
dim_provider  (60 rows, SCD Type 2 structure) -- SPECIALTY FLATTENED IN
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
  provider_key INT PK, provider_id, full_name, credential,
  specialty_id, specialty_name, specialty_code,          <-- flattened
  home_department_id, home_department_name,              <-- flattened
  effective_from, effective_to, is_current

  *** DEPARTURE FROM THE BRIEF ***
  Part 3.2 asks for specialty inside dim_provider AND a separate
  dim_specialty table. Building both is snowflaking -- normalising a
  dimension back out into sub-tables -- which is precisely what a star
  schema exists to avoid. dim_specialty is therefore NOT built.

  The cost of complying would be real: every specialty-grouped query (Q1,
  Q3, Q4 -- three of the four) would pay an extra join to recover an
  attribute that fits in one VARCHAR column on a 60-row table. The entire
  saving would be a few kilobytes of duplicated specialty names across 60
  rows.

  Redundancy in a dimension is not a defect in dimensional modelling. It
  is the trade being made on purpose.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------
dim_department  (12 rows, SCD Type 1)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
  department_key INT PK, department_id, department_name, floor, capacity

  Kept as a genuine dimension even though dim_provider already carries the
  provider's home department. They answer different questions: "which
  department did this care happen in" (a property of the encounter, and
  its own FK on encounters) versus "which department does this clinician
  belong to" (a property of the provider). They are equal in the current
  dataset, but modelling them as one would make it impossible to represent
  a cardiologist covering a shift in the ER -- an entirely normal event.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------
dim_diagnosis  (40 rows) and dim_procedure  (30 rows)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
  diagnosis_key PK, diagnosis_id, icd10_code, icd10_description,
                    icd10_chapter (derived from the code's first letter)
  procedure_key PK, procedure_id, cpt_code, cpt_description,
                    cpt_category (derived from the code range)

  Required by the bridges (Decision 4). The derived chapter/category
  columns are added because they cost nothing at 40 and 30 rows and they
  give analysts a roll-up level the source has no table for.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------
NOT BUILT: dim_encounter_type
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
  *** DEPARTURE FROM THE BRIEF ***
  Three values -- 'Outpatient', 'Inpatient', 'ER'. A dimension table this
  small buys a join and returns nothing: no hierarchy, no additional
  attributes, no roll-up. encounter_type is stored directly on the fact
  row as a DEGENERATE dimension.

  Same treatment for claim_status ('Paid' / 'Pending' / 'Denied') and for
  encounter_id itself, which is the classic degenerate dimension -- a
  natural key with no attributes of its own, kept on the fact for
  drill-through back to the source system.

SUMMARY OF DEPARTURES
  dim_specialty ....... dropped, flattened into dim_provider (anti-snowflake)
  dim_encounter_type .. dropped, kept as a degenerate attribute
  age_group ........... moved from dim_patient to the fact row (time-variance)
  dim_diagnosis ....... added (the brief lists it only as a question)
  dim_procedure ....... added (same)


=======================================================================
DECISION 3: PRE-AGGREGATED METRICS ON THE FACT TABLE
=======================================================================

FOREIGN KEYS
  date_key, discharge_date_key, patient_key, provider_key,
  department_key, principal_diagnosis_key

DEGENERATE DIMENSIONS
  encounter_id, encounter_type, claim_status

MEASURES
  encounter_count .......... always 1. Lets every count be a SUM, so all
                             measures aggregate identically.
  length_of_stay_minutes ... computed once at load from the two datetimes
  patient_age_years ........ age at the encounter date (see Decision 2)
  diagnosis_count .......... rows in the diagnosis bridge for this encounter
  procedure_count .......... rows in the procedure bridge
  claim_amount, allowed_amount, denied_amount
  is_readmission_30d ....... 0/1, precomputed
  is_index_admission ....... 0/1, inpatient with a discharge date

JUSTIFICATION, measure by measure

  diagnosis_count / procedure_count
  These are the counts that Q2's join was computing the expensive way. Any
  question of the form "how many diagnoses does an average ER visit carry"
  becomes AVG(diagnosis_count) against a single table -- no bridge, no
  join, no fan. The bridge is then needed only when the specific CODES
  matter, not merely how many there were.

  total allowed / claim / denied
  Collapses the billing join entirely. In the OLTP schema Q4 must join
  billing -> encounters before it can sum anything. Here the money is
  already on the encounter row.

  Note this also defends against source defect B7: billing.encounter_id is
  not UNIQUE, so a double-billed encounter would be double-counted by a
  naive join. The ETL aggregates billing to encounter grain, so the defect
  is neutralised once at load rather than in every query.

  is_readmission_30d
  The single highest-value precomputation here. Q3's entire self-join --
  30,000 index probes plus date-range evaluation -- is executed ONCE, by
  the ETL, and the answer is stored as a flag. Q3 collapses to a GROUP BY
  over a stored column.

  This is the real lesson of the whole exercise: the star schema is not
  faster because stars are magic. It is faster because the expensive work
  was moved from query time to load time, where it happens once a day
  instead of once per analyst per question.

  length_of_stay_minutes
  TIMESTAMPDIFF on every row at query time, replaced by a stored integer.
  Also the place to enforce source defect B8 -- nothing in the OLTP schema
  prevents discharge_date < encounter_date, so the ETL rejects negative
  values rather than propagating them.

  patient_age_years
  As above: time-variant, so it belongs to the event, not the person.

  principal_diagnosis_key
  Denormalised onto the fact from the diagnosis with sequence = 1. Most
  clinical reporting only wants the primary diagnosis, and this lets those
  queries avoid the bridge completely. Encounters with secondary diagnoses
  still have them in the bridge; this is a shortcut for the common case,
  not a replacement.

WHAT IS DELIBERATELY NOT PRE-AGGREGATED
  No monthly or specialty-level totals are stored on the fact table.
  Those are roll-ups of the fact, not properties of an encounter, and
  storing them would violate the declared grain. Where a roll-up genuinely
  needs to be materialised, it belongs in a separate aggregate table --
  which is exactly what agg_diagnosis_procedure_pair is (Decision 4).


=======================================================================
DECISION 4: BRIDGE TABLES
=======================================================================

DECISION: Yes. Two bridges, plus one aggregate table.

  bridge_encounter_diagnoses  (encounter_key, diagnosis_key,
                               diagnosis_sequence, is_principal)
  bridge_encounter_procedures (encounter_key, procedure_key,
                               procedure_date_key)

WHY BRIDGES ARE NECESSARY

The alternative is denormalising diagnoses and procedures into the fact
table, and there are only two ways to do that, both bad:

  (a) Repeating groups -- diagnosis_1, diagnosis_2, ... diagnosis_5.
      Breaks the moment a patient presents with six. Makes "how many
      encounters involved hypertension" a five-way OR across five columns.
      This is a First Normal Form violation, and 1NF is not something a
      star schema licenses you to abandon.

  (b) Change the grain to diagnosis level -- which is Decision 1's Option
      B, and reintroduces the exact 6.9x revenue inflation measured in Q2.

A bridge keeps the many-to-many where it belongs: outside the fact table,
at its own grain, joined only by queries that actually need codes.

THE TRADE-OFF, STATED HONESTLY

Bridges do not eliminate the fan trap. They relocate it. A query joining
both bridges still produces ~2.06M rows, because pairing diagnoses with
procedures inherently produces pairs -- that is the question Q2 asks, and
no schema can answer it without forming them.

What bridges DO buy:
  - The fan is opt-in. It happens only in queries that join a bridge.
    Q1, Q3 and Q4 never touch one and cannot be affected.
  - The rows are narrow. Bridge rows are 3-4 integers; the OLTP junction
    join dragged VARCHAR(200) descriptions through the sort. Sorting 2M
    narrow rows is far cheaper than sorting 2M wide ones.
  - No measure is reachable. Money lives on the fact table, which the
    bridge-to-bridge join does not include. The 6.9x inflation is
    structurally unreachable rather than merely discouraged.

That last point is the strongest argument. A schema where the wrong answer
is impossible to write beats a schema where it is merely documented.

AND THE HONEST ANSWER FOR Q2: AN AGGREGATE TABLE

Even with narrow bridges, forming 2M pairs to return 20 rows is wasteful
when the question is asked repeatedly. So the ETL also materialises:

  agg_diagnosis_procedure_pair (diagnosis_key, procedure_key,
                                encounter_count, ...)  -- ~1,200 rows

Q2 then reads 1,200 pre-computed rows instead of building 2,060,000.

This is Kimball's aggregate navigation, and it must be reported honestly:
the resulting speedup comes from PRECOMPUTATION, not from the star shape.
Claiming the star schema itself made Q2 fast would be false. What the star
schema provides is a clean, stable grain to aggregate FROM -- building the
same table off the OLTP schema would be possible but far more fragile.

WOULD I DO IT DIFFERENTLY IN PRODUCTION?

Three changes:

  1. Add a diagnosis WEIGHTING FACTOR to the bridge (1/n per encounter),
     the standard Kimball technique for making bridged measures safely
     allocatable. Not needed for these four questions; needed the moment
     someone asks "revenue attributable to diabetes".
  2. Make the bridges SCD-aware if diagnosis coding can be revised after
     the fact -- clinical coding is frequently amended post-discharge.
  3. Partition fact_encounters by date_key. At 300,000 rows it is
     unnecessary; at 300 million it is the difference between a working
     warehouse and a broken one.
